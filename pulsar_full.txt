Task General Description
This section provides an overview of the task.
For each task, you are given an audio file. Your ultimate goal is to generate a spoken-form
transcription of the audio. Spoken-form transcriptions have the following characteristics,
among others:
● Letters are in lowercase only.
● Punctuation is limited (see rules in the instructions).
● Numbers are written as words (e.g., three).
Note: Spoken-form transcription does NOT mean that you should use the phonetic spelling.
Always transcribe according to the spelling in the reference dictionary (Appendix 1).
To process a task, follow the below steps:
1. Listen to the recording. Just play the clip, don't type. Focus on absorbing the overall
meaning (topic, speakers). Context can help you figure out what to transcribe,
especially if there are unclear words or homophones.
2. Transcribe the audio. Play the audio in chunks, pausing to type what you heard. If
you're not sure about how to deal with a specific phenomenon, look for and read the
relative section in these instructions. If you encounter unclear audio segments, you can
replay, slow down the audio, or use context which often helps to provide the correct
transcription.
3. Write your transcription in the Transcribe the audio text box.
4. Indicate the number of the speaker(s) from the How many identified speakers does the
audio contain? question.
5. Indicate the gender of the speaker(s) from the Select the gender of the speaker(s)
question.
6. Indicate the nativity of the speaker(s) from the Select the nativity of the speaker(s) in
the audio file question.
7. Submit the task.
Attention: The use of Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) to produce transcriptions is
prohibited.
The following section specifies what are the most important aspects to take into account
during the transcription process and why.
Importance of Transcription Accuracy
Your transcription is the map between audio and text that will be used to train a text-to-speech
model. This means that your transcriptions are the signals that the model will use to learn the
association between audio and text. The model learns: "when I hear this sound, the correct
text is this." Every mismatch between what's in the audio and what you type teaches the
model something wrong. This impacts the end-product and erodes customer trust. That's why
you transcribe not just every word, but every "um"s and "oh"s, repeated words, and
non-speech sound - exactly as they are spoken.
There are three ways a transcription breaks that map. These are the errors most critical to the
model:
- Deletion - you leave out something that was said (a word or a tag, an "um," a repeat). The
model incorrectly learns "it's OK to ignore sounds in the audio," and in production, it drops real
words.
Audio: "i uh i think so" --> Wrong transcription: "i think so" / Correct transcription: "i uh i
think so"
- Hallucination: you add something that wasn't said (an extra word or an extra tag, a guess
typed as if certain). The model incorrectly learns "invent content to fill gaps," and in production,
it makes up words.
Audio: "we should probably h-home" --> Wrong transcription: "we should probably go
home" / Correct transcription: "we should probably h- home"
- Substitution: you write a different word than what was said (mishearing, "fixing" grammar,
normalizing something you shouldn't). The model learns the wrong sound-to-text mapping.
Audio: "he don't like it" -> Wrong transcription: "he doesn't like it" / Correct transcription:
"he don't like it"
Rule of thumb: If you read only your transcription, it should account for everything that was
said in the audio and nothing that wasn't — same words, same fillers, same order. When in
doubt, transcribe what you hear, not what you expect.

Unintelligible and Guessed Words and Phrases
Audio can be noisy, and it is not always easy to grasp the speech content in an audio file. This
section provides the guidance for handling unclear audio, where you can not clearly determine
the accurate transcription. It is important to clearly and accurately mark the presence and
location of this unintelligibility, in order to maximize usefulness of the data for the model.
General guidance: Use double parentheses (()) for speech that is unintelligible. A single (())
can cover one or multiple unintelligible words. Place it where the speech occurs. Do not include
any space inside the parentheses. Use a space to separate (()) from adjacent words or tags.
Specific guidance:
● Do use (()) where speech is genuinely unintelligible. Do NOT use it where speech is
clearly intelligible. Consider a word as genuinely unintelligible if it takes multiple listens,
slowing the audio, or repeated verification to identify.
● Always attempt a best guess inside the parentheses when audio is even partially
intelligible, including truncated words. An uncertain guess is more valuable than empty
(()).
● Use empty (()) only when speech is completely indiscernible after multiple listens — no
syllables, no guessable words.
● Use (()) for problems with the speech itself (unknown language, confusing words), not
for audio quality issues — use the Garbled Audio tag for those. Research unfamiliar
words before marking them unintelligible.
● If the entire file is unintelligible speech from the main speaker, insert (()) only.
● For background unintelligible speech, refer to the Background Speech section.

Backchannels and Filled Pauses
What are backchannels and filled pauses and why are they important? Backchannels are
signals that a listener produces during a conversation to signal that they are
following/understanding/reacting to what the other person says, including non-verbal words
for affirmations, negations, or thinking. These lack standardized spellings, so here we align on
the normalization rules, so that the model will get a coherent signal from the transcription data.
Backchannels and filled pauses are important components in natural, real-time
communication, so we want the transcription to capture backchannels and filled pauses
with maximum accuracy, for the model to be able to perform deep understanding of the
user's speech and respond accordingly. Do NOT treat backchannels and filled pauses as
secondary elements.
General guidance: Transcribe backchannels using the standardized spellings in the reference
table below. Use the [fp] tag for filled pauses not listed in the table. See the "Backchannels and
Filled Pauses ([fp] tag)" section
Specific guidance:
● Do not replace non-verbal sounds in catalog entities (song titles, brand names, etc.)
with a backchannel word.

Text Normalization
What is text normalization and why it is important: Text normalization is the process of
converting different formats of text into standardized representations. Text normalization can
be done on several elements. One example is numbers: One can transcribe numbers using
digits or using words. Normalizing means deciding on one specific format to be used
consistently across all instances. We normalize in order to avoid providing mixed signals to the
model which can lead to unreliable behavior in the end-product.
Numbers
Transcribe numbers using words, not digits. Numbers include numerals, ordinals (like 1st),
quantities, times, sequences, distances, and arithmetic.
● Transcribe letters in codes and sequences with a following period (e.g., "a. b. c.", "u. a.").
Examples (spoken form):
- eighty-seven
- fifth
- three zero six
- three oh six
- what is five plus five plus five plus five plus five
- my confirmation code is a. b. c. one two three
- the flight number is u. a. seven twenty-nine
- the pie costs five dollars

Letter Sequences
Letter sequences are spelled-out words or individual letters spoken in an audio file.
● Add a period, then a space, following each individual letter.
● Do NOT use a period after one-letter words (such as "i" in English and Italian, or "y" in
Spanish and French).
Examples:
my name is marco alvarez a. l. v. a. r. e. z.

Titles and Catalog Entities
General guidance: A catalog entity is a titled or branded list item an agent could search for
during entity resolution — books, movies, songs, albums, playlists, artists, apps, skills,
channels, cities, countries, and so on. Transcriptions follow all standard transcription rules.
(Note: language-specific guidance on titles and catalog entities appears in other sections; refer
to the conventions as a whole.)
Specific guidance:
● Transcribe titles and catalog entities by their official spelling when possible, including
hyphenation.
● Omit disallowed characters when pronunciation isn't affected; if a character replaces a
letter, transcribe the letter (e.g., P!nk → pink, Ke$ha → kesha).
● Transcribe numbers and abbreviations as full words (e.g., I Would Die 4 U → i would die
four u; Alien vs. Predator → alien versus predator).
● Normalize a branded spelling when it isn't pronounceable as branded or doesn't reflect
the user's pronunciation (e.g., CHVRCHES → churches).
● For a single letter connected to a word by a hyphen, transcribe the hyphen and do not
use a period + space (e.g., T-Pain → t-pain, Oral-B → oral-b).
● When a phrase could be a catalog entity or common words, use context. If it clearly
refers to a known entity, transcribe it as that entity (as close to branded spelling as
spoken-form rules allow), not split into phonetically similar words (e.g., U2 → u. two,
not "you two"; "play the northman" → the northman, not "the north men"). Research
when uncertain.

Plurals and Possessives of Letter Sequences
General guidance: Form plurals and possessives of acronyms by attaching the ending
immediately after the final period.
Specific guidance:
● For a plural, transcribe "s" immediately after the final period (e.g., "c. d.s").
● For a possessive, transcribe "'s" immediately after the final period (e.g., "i. b. m.'s").
Examples:

Contractions and Informal Contractions
General guidance
Core principle: Transcribe contractions as spoken. If the speaker uses a contracted form, write
the contraction. If the speaker clearly says the full/expanded form, write the full form. Why this
matters: Transcriptions are used to train speech recognition systems and to align text with
audio. Using the correct form, contracted or expanded, ensures the transcription accurately
reflects both the timing and structure of what was actually said.
When to use a contraction: Use a contraction when the speaker merges words together such
that the contracted form is the most natural and recognizable way to represent what was said.
For example:
● If "I am" is spoken as one syllable, transcribe as "i'm" - the two words have merged into
one unit.
● If "give me" is spoken without the /v/ sound (as a single blended word), transcribe as
"gimme" - writing "give me" would misrepresent what was actually spoken.
● if a possible contraction doesn't sound anything like the full expanded version (e.g.
“imma” doesn't sound anything like “I'm going to”) and there is a standard spelling of
that contraction (found in a reputable dictionary, even if it's not Merriam-Webster or
American Heritage Dictionary), then there is a good chance that the best judgment is to
go ahead and use the contraction.
When NOT to use a contraction: Use the expanded form when the speaker clearly articulates
the separate words, even if a contraction exists. For example:
● If you can clearly hear two distinct words "I am" with two syllables, transcribe as "i am"
- not "i'm."
● If the speaker clearly says "give me" with a distinct /v/, transcribe as "give me" - not
"gimme."
Steps to follow:
1. Hear a potential contraction → listen carefully and determine if the speaker is actually
using the contracted form (not clearly saying both words separately).
2. If contracted form is spoken → check if it's in the list below.
3. If it's in the list → use the contraction.
4. If expanded form is clearly spoken → use the expanded form, even if the contraction is
in the list.
5. If the contraction is NOT in the list → use your best judgment. If it's a well-established
contraction that accurately captures what was spoken, it may be acceptable. When in
doubt, prefer the expanded form.

Words with Same Pronunciation and Same Meaning
General guidance: When two variant spellings share the same pronunciation and meaning and
both appear in Merriam-Webster Dictionary, either form is acceptable. Labeler/verifier
misalignment on these variants is not an error (e.g., yup vs. yep).
Specific guidance:
● Accept either variant when both are dictionary-listed and share pronunciation and
meaning.
● Some apparent synonyms have distinct usage — use the form matching the speaker's
intended meaning: "all right" for correctness ("His answers were all right"), "alright" for
adequacy/satisfaction ("I'm feeling alright").
Examples:
Variant A Variant B Notes
yep yup Both acceptable
yeah yea Both acceptable

Hyphens
General guidance: Transcribe hyphens only in the specific cases listed below.

Tags
What are tags and why they are important: Tags are labels included in transcriptions to
indicate specific features of the audio, like cross-talk, laughing, non-lexical sounds, etc.
Indicating these specific features, with the correct tags and in the correct location where the
audio features occur, is critical for the model. Missing tags that are needed or adding tags
that shouldn't be added are critical error signals for the model, just like missing words or
adding words incorrectly. See the table below for the full list of tags.
General guidance: Some tags represent audio features that occur within speech; others
represent types of speech.
Specific guidance:
● When multiple tags are needed, list them in alphabetical order, each separated by a
single space. Do not add a space before a tag that begins the text or after a tag that
ends the text.
● Main and background speech tags apply only to human utterances.
● Place [fp], [hn], and unintelligible markers (()) in their defined positions. Flag ambiguous
placement for overturn review.
● For single-speaker audio, do not include the <s1> tag.

Non-Lexical Vocal Sounds
General guidance: Non-lexical vocal sounds are noises a speaker makes that can't be
represented with standard spelling. For identifiable speakers, use the explicit tag [laughter]
and use [hn] for any other (e.g., yawn, cry, cough, throat-clear, sneeze, lip-smack, sigh).
Indicate these sounds in files with or without speech; if a file contains only such sounds, enter
only the relevant tag.
Specific guidance:
● Do NOT use [hn] for regular breathing sound; [hn] is used for noticeable sharp
inhalation, exhalation, yawns, cries, or other non-breathing non-lexical vocal sounds
not covered by other tags.
● Place the tag where the sound occurs. With multiple speakers, keep the tag with the
speaker who made the noise.
● If unsure whether the main speaker made the noise, assume they did.
● Represent multiple sounds in succession with a single tag; for ongoing sounds, place
one tag where it begins.
● When a third unidentifiable person makes a sound between two speakers, place the
[bg] tag at the end of the previous speaker's turn.
● If a sound interrupts a word, place the tag within the word and mark the break per
truncation rules (e.g., ama- [hn] -zing).
● If an identifiable speaker makes a sound while an identified speaker talks, use
overlapping speech tags <ol>.
● Use [bg] for background speech with non-lexical sounds from unidentifiable speakers,
whether it is laughter, unintelligible background speech, coughing, throat-clearing, etc.
● If an unidentifiable speaker makes a sound while an identified speaker talks, place [bg]
after the word it occurred during.

Background Speech
General guidance: The [bg] tag indicates background speech that cannot be attributed to an
identified speaker — speech too distant or unclear to assign to a specific person (e.g., a distant
voice in a crowd or an unidentifiable voice in another room). Use [bg] regardless of the spoken
language. [bg] is also used for non-lexical sounds from unidentifiable speakers (see
Non-Lexical Vocal Sounds)
Specific guidance:
● Use [bg] only when you cannot determine who is speaking.
● If you can identify a distinct speaker, use their speaker tag even if they aren't part of the
main conversation. If you can identify them but can't understand the content, use their
speaker tag with the Unintelligible Speech tag.
● A previously identified speaker keeps their speaker tag even if they become temporarily
unintelligible — do not use [bg] for them.
● Place [bg] where the unattributable speech occurs. For continuous background speech,
place [bg] at each point it becomes noticeable, not just at the start and end.
● Use one [bg] tag for multiple unidentifiable speakers in a single instance. If it occurs
during an identified speaker's turn, place [bg] after the interrupted speech.
● Absence of a wake word does not by itself mean speech is background speech — judge
by the audio context.

No Speech
General guidance: When there is no speech in the entire audio file, transcribe only the <ns>
tag. Accurate no-speech tagging teaches the model not to fill gaps with hallucinated content.
Specific guidance:
● Use <ns> only when no other tag applies — including audio with faint white noise but no
speech.
● If there is no speech but another tag applies, use that tag instead.
● This tag does not apply if there is any speech or noise associated with another tag,
including speech with prolonged pauses.
● Do not use any tag to indicate a pause in speech.

conversations: user descriptors.
Sensitive Content
When the content in the audio is sensitive, such as containing curse words or explicit content
(depending on annotator judgement), use <sc> as the tag. There is no set criteria that qualifies
sensitive content for this workflow. Use your best judgement to determine if the utterance is
sensitive. The purpose of the workflow is not to identify sensitive content, this option collects
general data and trends for the customer.
User Descriptors
What are user descriptors and how they are important? User descriptors allow us to capture
metadata about the speakers involved in the dialogue. Like tags, these are useful for the model
to obtain a clear understanding of what it's happening in the audio.
Gender
Indicate the gender of each speaker.
Use the following definitions to determine the gender of the speakers in an audio file.
● Masculine-Sounding: The speaker has a masculine-sounding voice.
● Feminine-Sounding: The speaker has a feminine-sounding voice.
● Unknown: The speaker has a voice which is ambiguous, e.g., toddlers, kids.
If you cannot determine the gender of the user due to voice distortion, do not guess. Select
Unknown.
Nativity
Indicate the nativity of each identified speaker in the audio file. Consider speakers with accents
for English-speaking countries as native speakers (including US, British, Canadian, Scottish,
Australian, Welsh).
Use the following definitions to determine the nativity of the speakers in an utterance:
● Native: Native speakers of English
● Non-Native: Non-native speakers of English
● Unknown: The nativity of all speakers is unknown or both native and non-native
speakers are present.
● TTS: The speaker(s) are computer-generated (agent) speech or TTS.
NOTE: If you cannot determine the nativity of the user due to voice distortion, do not guess.
Select Unknown. (Some Transcription service orders use intentionally distorted audio to
obscure identifying details about users.)
To classify a speaker as native or non-native, intelligible speech must be present from that
speaker. If a speaker only produces non-verbal expressions (e.g., laughter, crying, coughing,
sighing) without any intelligible speech, designate their nativity as Unknown.
This section concludes the description of specific aspects. The next sections will provide some
more general guidance, detailing some good practices and a glossary of definitions.
Research Best Practices
It is important to make sure that entity spellings are correct.
Search online to check the spelling of any entities you are not sure of. If you do not know where
to start, sound out the word or words. Enter them as best you can. Include key words that
might help direct the search (such as "town," "song," "movie," or "actor").
For example, if the user asks for music by the Bee Gees and you have never heard of the group,
you might search "BGs." If you do not include search terms related to music, the search will not
find the right spelling.
Once you add a music-related term, the search results redirect to the music group the Bee
Gees, even though the spelling of the search term is incorrect.
Glossary
● Audio feature: A sound that generates a data point for an audio file. Usually indicated
with a tag.
● Audio file: Specific instance of audio data that may include speech and other audio
features. Also called an utterance.
● Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR): The process by which the device interprets
users’ speech.
● Backchannel: A brief listener response such as "yeah," "right," or "uh huh" that signals
attention or acknowledgment without constituting a full turn. Backchannels typically
occur while another speaker is talking.
● Catalog entity: A titled or branded list item. Examples include books, movies, songs,
albums, playlists, artists, apps, skills, channels, cities, and countries. A catalog entity is
something that an agent could search for during entity resolution.
● Cross talk: Unintelligible, overlapping speech from multiple speakers.
● Data source: The situation from which audio data originates.
● Dictionary of record: The spelling reference for the target language.
● Entity resolution: The process of identifying, linking, and merging disparate data
records that refer to the same real-world entity (such as a person, organization, or
product).
● Homophone: A word that sounds the same as another word but has a different
meaning.
● Homonym: A word that is spelled the same as another word but has a different
meaning.
● Intelligible speech: Speech that you are able to understand and transcribe.
● Main Speaker: The main person speaking in the utterance.
● Metadata: Data associated with a file (for example, Gender and Nativity) that is not part
of the transcribed text.
● Non-target language: A language that is not supported or expected for a given locale.
● Stream: A single, continuous audio file; may include several utterances.
● Tag: A standard series of letters and characters that indicates an audio feature.
● Target language: The language that is supported or expected for a given locale.
● Truncation: The act of cutting off a word or sound before its intended end. Truncated
audio often occurs at the beginning or end of the audio file.
● Turn: A continuous stretch of speech by one speaker, bounded by another speaker's
speech, a pause, or the start or end of the audio file.
● Unintelligible speech: Speech that you cannot understand or transcribe.
● User: The speaker in an audio file.
● Verifier: A Data associate who audits transcriptions for quality.

was like gesundheit"). If you understand the language, transcribe as much as you can. Only use
accents and diacritics that are part of the target language. If a word includes an accent that is
not part of the target language, transcribe the closest plain letter. Insert double parentheses to
represent sections of non-target language speech that you do not understand.
Do NOT transcribe non-target languages in background speech. Use the [bg] tag to indicate
non-target languages that occur in background speech.
If you are unable to transcribe any of a non-target language utterance, insert double
parentheses.
If the entire utterance is unintelligible non-target language, use <nt>.
Non-Target Language Dictionaries
When you encounter a word in a non-target language, and if the words/phrases are reasonably
used within the target language (e.g. "he sneezed and i was like gesundheit"), use that
non-target language's dictionary to confirm the correct spelling. Adapt the spelling to fit your
locale's allowed characters:
● Remove diacritics if your locale does not allow them. For example, "medellín"
becomes "medellin" in English locales because accented characters are not permitted.
● Use Latin characters only if your locale does not allow non-Latinate scripts. For
example, if a speaker says a word in Russian or Arabic, write it using Latin characters
rather than Cyrillic or Arabic script.
If the word does not appear in the relevant dictionary, use Google to confirm the spelling.

Cross-language homophones
A cross-language homophone is a word with the same pronunciation in both the target
language and a non-target language. An example is the English shoe and German schuh. If a
cross-language homophone occurs in isolation, assume it is part of the target language.
A foreign-origin word is part of the target language only if the user’s meaning matches the
meaning in the dictionary. For instance, some non-English dictionaries include the English word
“home” as it refers to technology (such as a home screen or a home page). If a user in a
non-English locale uses the word “home” to refer to the place where one lives, this usage is not
part of the target language.

Location Names
General guidance: When transcribing names of cities, towns, and other locations, consider the
locale the data comes from.
Specific guidance:
● Some locations are homophones — include locale context in your search to find the
correct spelling.
● Let the source device's locale guide your choice between homophone spellings.
